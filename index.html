Prepositions of place

For places, think of in as inside a space, on as on a surface or a line, and at as a point on the map. For movement, the preposition shows the direction.

In short
in = inside a space · on = on a surface or a line · at = a point, or where something happens.

in

inside a space, an area

in the box · in the kitchen · in Santiago · in Chile · in the car · in a taxi · in the photo · in bed

on

a surface, a line, public transport

on the table · on the wall · on the floor · on the 3rd floor · on the bus / train / plane · on Main Street (US) · on the coast

at

a point, a place where something happens

at home · at work · at school · at the door · at the bus stop · at a party · at the doctor's · at 123 Main St.

Same place, different idea: I'm at the cinema (that's where I am) vs it's cold in the cinema (inside the building). And transport: in a car or taxi, but on a bus, train or plane.

Position

next to / beside al lado de
The bank is next to the pharmacy.

between entre (dos)
I sit between Ana and Tom.

in front of delante de · behind detrás de
There's a park in front of my building.

opposite / across from frente a, al otro lado
The café is opposite the station.

above / over encima de · below / under debajo de
The cat is under the bed.

near cerca de · far from lejos de
I live near the metro. near of

Used to, be used to, get used to

They look almost the same, but they mean three different things. The trick: after be used to and get used to comes a noun or verb + -ing.

In short
used to + verb = past habit (solía) · be used to + -ing = it's normal for you · get used to + -ing = it's becoming normal.
used to + verb

Past habits that stopped

solía, antes…

  • I used to smoke. (not anymore)
  • We used to live in Temuco.
  • I didn't use to like coffee.
  • Did you use to play football?
be used to + noun / -ing

It's normal for you

estar acostumbrado a

  • I'm used to waking up early.
  • She's used to the cold.
  • I'm not used to driving on the left.
get used to + noun / -ing

It's becoming normal

acostumbrarse a

  • I'm getting used to my new job.
  • You'll get used to it.
  • It took me a year to get used to living alone.
would also works for repeated actions in the past, but not for states: When I was a kid, we would go to the beach every summer · I used to have a dog · I would have a dog.
I'm used to wake up early → I'm used to waking up early. In the negative and in questions, it's use to (no -d): I didn't use to · I didn't used to.

Questions, requests and time words

Questions: normal word order

"Where do you live?" → She asked me where I lived.
"Are you OK?" → He asked if I was OK.

Requests and orders

"Please wait." → He asked me to wait.

Time and place words

The verb to be

One verb, two meanings in Spanish: ser and estar. Use it for identity, feelings, location, age and descriptions.

In short
be = ser and estar. Use it for age and feelings too: I'm 30, I'm hungry (not "I have").

Do vs make

Both mean hacer. The quick rule: do is for activities and tasks; make is for creating or producing something. Then there are collocations you just learn.

In short
do = activities and tasks (do homework) · make = create or produce something (make dinner, make a decision).
do

Work, jobs and tasks

do the housework, do your homework, do a good job, do your chores

Vague activities

do something, do nothing, do anything, do everything

When the action is obvious

do your hair, do the dishes, do the laundry, do an exam

Also

do exercise, do business, do me a favor, do your best

make

Food and drinks

make breakfast, make dinner, make a cake, make a coffee

Plans and decisions

make a decision, make a plan, make a choice, make arrangements

Speaking and sounds

make a comment, make a suggestion, make a noise, make a speech, make a call

Reactions and results

make you happy, make you laugh, make a mistake, make money, make friends, make progress

Material and origin

made of wood, made from grapes, made in China

The classic one: I made a mistake · I did a mistake

In, on, at for time

Think of it as big → medium → small. In for long periods, on for days, at for exact moments.

In short
in = long periods (in May) · on = days and dates (on Monday) · at = exact times (at 3:00). Nothing before next, last, this or every.

in

months, years, seasons, parts of the day

in October · in 2026 · in summer · in the morning · in the afternoon · in the evening · in two weeks

on

days and dates

on Monday · on Mondays · on March 28th · on my birthday · on Christmas Day · on the weekend (US)

at

exact times

at 3 o'clock · at 4:20 · at noon · at midnight · at night · at the moment · at the weekend (UK)

Watch out: in the morning, but at night. And no preposition with this, next, last, every: next Monday · on next Monday

To vs for

Both can be para or a in Spanish, which is why they're confusing. Here's when you use each one.

In short
to = destination, who receives it, purpose + verb (I'm here to learn) · for = benefit, duration, purpose + noun (for dinner).
to
  • Destination: I'm going to Bogotá next week.
  • Telling time: It's a quarter to nine. (9 menos cuarto)
  • Distance: It's 5 km from my house to the store.
  • Who receives it: I gave the book to her.
  • Purpose + verb: I came here to see her. (para verla)
for
  • Benefit: Running is good for your health.
  • Duration: We've lived here for five years.
  • What it's used for: What are these keys for?
  • Who it's meant for: This call is for you.
  • Doing it instead of someone: Can you do the dishes for me?
  • Purpose + noun: Let's go out for dinner.
The big one: purpose with a verb is to, never for. I'm here to learn · I'm here for learn

Sentence basics

English is strict about word order. Spanish lets you move things around; English mostly doesn't.

In short

Gerund or infinitive

Gerund = verb + -ing, used like a noun: I enjoy reading. Reading helps you learn.

Summary helper

Use this when you tell me about the podcast, video or story you listened to or read. Go in this order and you'll never get lost.

In short
What it was → main idea → 2–3 details → your reaction → what you didn't get.
1 · what

Say what it was

  • I listened to an episode of … about…
  • I read a story called …
  • It's a podcast where two people talk about…
2 · main idea

Give the big picture

  • It's basically about…
  • The main point was that…
  • In short, they were talking about…
3 · details

Add two or three details

  • First, they explained…
  • Then they mentioned that…
  • One example they gave was…
  • At the end, …
4 · you

Give your reaction

  • What surprised me was…
  • I (didn't) agree with…
  • It made me think about…
  • It reminded me of…
5 · honest

Say what you didn't get

  • I didn't totally get the part where…
  • There was a word I didn't know: …
  • They spoke really fast when…

Example

I listened to an episode of 6 Minute English about sleep. It's basically about why teenagers sleep so late. First, they explained that their body clock changes. Then they mentioned that schools should start later. What surprised me was that teenagers need nine hours. I didn't totally get the part where they talked about melatonin.

Use the past simple for what happened in the episode (they explained, she said) and the present simple for what it's about (it's about, the main idea is).

  • Yes and no.
  • I agree up to a point.

  • I'm not sure about that.
  • I see it a bit differently.
  • I don't really agree, because…
  • Maybe, but have you thought about…?

Ask for opinions

  • What do you think?

Conditionals

Pick an example or write your own. The four conditionals update below so you can compare them side by side.

In short
If + present, will = real future · If + past, would = imaginary · If + had done, would have = past that didn't happen. Never will or would in the if part.
If
,
The if part can go first or second. If it goes first, use a comma: If I have time, I'll call you. · I'll call you if I have time.
Never put will or would in the if part: If I have time · If I will have time

Present perfect vs past simple

The question to ask yourself: do I say (or know) when it happened? If yes, past simple. If it's about your life until now, present perfect.

In short
Do you say when? → past simple (I went last year). Life until now, no time → present perfect (I've been there twice).
past simple · I went

Finished time

  • I went to Brazil last year.
  • She called me yesterday.
  • We lived in Valpo when I was a kid.

yesterday · last week · in 2019 · ago · when…

present perfect · I have gone

Life until now, no specific time

  • I've been to Brazil twice.
  • She hasn't called me yet.
  • We've lived here for five years (and still do).

ever · never · already · yet · just · for · since · so far

How they work together

Have you ever been to Europe? (experiencia, sin fecha)

Yes, I have. I went to Spain in 2022. (ahora sí hay fecha → past simple)

I have seen him yesterday → I saw him yesterday. With a finished time word, always use the past simple.
For vs since: for + a period (for two years), since + a starting point (since 2020, since Monday). I live here since 2020 → I've lived here since 2020

-ing or to?

When one verb comes after another, the second one takes one of two forms: the gerund (verb + -ing) or the infinitive (to + verb). Which one depends on the first verb.

In short
-ing after prepositions and verbs like enjoy, avoid, finish · to + verb after want, need, decide, hope.
gerund · verb + -ing

What it is

A verb that works like a noun, the name of an activity. En español casi siempre se traduce con el infinitivo: nadar.

How to make it

  • Base verb + -ing: read → reading, go → going
  • Drop the final -e: make → making
  • Double the last letter in short verbs: stop → stopping, swim → swimming
  • -ie becomes -y: lie → lying, die → dying

When to use it

  • As the subject: Swimming is good for you.
  • After certain verbs: I enjoy swimming.
  • After every preposition: I'm good at swimming.
infinitive · to + verb

What it is

The basic form of the verb with to in front. Es el infinitivo en español: nadar, pero con "to".

How to make it

  • to + base verb: to swim, to go, to be
  • Never add -s, -ed or -ing after to: to go · to goes · to went
  • Negative: not to + verb: I decided not to go.

When to use it

  • After certain verbs: I want to swim.
  • To say why (purpose): I came to learn. (para aprender)
  • After adjectives: It's easy to use.
Don't mix them up with the continuous tenses: in I am swimming, the -ing is part of the tense (am + swimming). A gerund is the -ing form used as a thing or activity: I love swimming. And after modals (can, should, must…) and let / make, use the verb with no to: I can swim · I can to swim.

Comparatives & superlatives

Comparative = comparing two things (bigger than). Superlative = the top one of a group (the biggest).

In short
Short adjectives: -er / the -est (cheaper, the cheapest). Long ones: more / the most. good → better → the best.

Irregular ones

bad → worse → the worst

a lot / many / much → more → the most

Other ways to compare

as … as

She's as tall as me. (tan alta como)
It's not as hot as yesterday.

less … than

This one is less expensive than that one.

Make it stronger

much better · a lot cheaper · way easier (informal) · a bit slower

more better · more cheaper. Use -er or more, never both. And it's than, not that: bigger than

Numbers, dates & money

English uses dots and commas the opposite way from Chile. This trips people up in meetings all the time.

In short
English uses a point for decimals and commas for thousands: 1.5 = one point five · 10,000 = ten thousand.

Decimals and thousands

  • 1.5 = one point five (en Chile: 1,5)
  • 10,000 = ten thousand (en Chile: 10.000)
  • 2,500,000 = two point five million / two million five hundred thousand
  • 0.25 = zero point two five

Money

  • $15.99 = fifteen ninety-nine
  • $250 = two hundred and fifty dollars (UK) / two hundred fifty (US)
  • $10.000 CLP = ten thousand pesos
  • US$1M = one million dollars (no “millions”)

Years

  • 1998 = nineteen ninety-eight
  • 2005 = two thousand five
  • 2026 = twenty twenty-six
  • the 90s = the nineties

Dates

  • US: 10/21/2026 = October twenty-first
  • UK: 21/10/2026 = the twenty-first of October
  • Use ordinals: 1st, 2nd, 3rd, 4th, 21st, 22nd, 23rd
  • on October 21st · in October · in 2026

Time

  • 7:15 = seven fifteen / a quarter past seven
  • 7:30 = seven thirty / half past seven
  • 7:45 = seven forty-five / a quarter to eight
  • 9 am · 3 pm · noon · midnight

Other numbers

  • 25% = twenty-five percent
  • ½ ⅓ ¼ = a half, a third, a quarter
  • +56 9 1234 5678 → say digits one by one: nine, one, two, three, four…
  • 0 = zero, or “oh” in phone numbers
13 vs 30: thirTEEN (stress at the end) vs THIRty (stress at the start). Same with 14/40, 15/50… Make the difference clear or someone will pay the wrong price.
two hundred people · two hundreds people. Hundred, thousand and million don't take -s after a number.
